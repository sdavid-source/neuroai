What the Number Does

The ethics of measuring intelligence, as a pipeline — and why machines are running it again

The claim: ethical failure in intelligence measurement is not a property of bad tests. It is what happens when a measure reaches allocation before its construct validity is settled. Human psychometrics made that journey between 1905 and 1927.

Every intelligence measurement — a child's, a recruit's, a defendant's, a model's — runs the same seven stages. Something is posited as the thing being measured; a task is chosen to stand in for it; a reference population is picked; many things become one number; the number acquires a meaning; the meaning decides something about someone; and then the decision changes the thing being measured.

Harms do not attach to the test. They attach to stages, and they attach in a consistent pattern: the trouble starts when stage six arrives before stages one to three are finished. So this map is organised by stage rather than by harm, which means you can check the claim rather than take my word for it — and it means the machine column can be read against the human one directly. The machine column has just reached stage six.

One thing this map is not: an argument against measurement. The Flynn effect was the instrument refuting the strongest claims made for it. Gender Shades exposed a harm that was invisible until someone built a better benchmark. The APA task force was a field publishing its own uncertainty. Measurement is both the mechanism of the damage and the only instrument that finds it, and a map that showed you only the first half would be doing the thing it criticises.

    How I built this, and what I left out

    The problem with the topic

    “The ethics of measuring intelligence” has an obvious failure mode: it becomes an opinion essay with a navigation bar. The organising principle ends up being “here are some bad things,” which is a list, not a map — and a reader cannot tell my curation apart from my politics.

    So I looked for a structure that would make the ethics mechanical rather than editorial. The seven-stage pipeline is that structure. Every measurement of intelligence passes through all seven stages whether or not anyone names them, and harms enter at identifiable points. Organising by stage means the map makes a checkable claim about how measurement works, and the ethics falls out of the structure instead of being asserted on top of it.

    Why the layout is two bands

    Human psychometrics on top, machine benchmarks below, sharing one horizontal axis of stages. That shared axis is the entire argument: it lets you read the two columns against each other and see that the human band has markers all the way through stage six, while the machine band clusters at stages one to four and has only just begun to populate stage six. You can see the thesis before you read a single label. If the machine band's stage-six column fills in over the next few years, this map predicts what follows, and it will be checkable against what actually happens.

    The load-bearing element

    The Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan impossibility result is the one thing on the map that is a proof rather than an event, and it is why the project is a structural finding rather than a moral position. Calibration within groups and equal error rates across groups cannot both hold when base rates differ. That means “just make the test fair” is not an available instruction. Fairness at stage four is a choice between incompatible criteria, made by someone, and it cannot be engineered away — which is exactly why the ethical work has to happen at stage six, where you decide what the score is permitted to decide. It is drawn differently from every other marker because it applies to every row.

    What I cut, and why

    • The heritability literature as a topic in itself. It appears once, as a specific interpretation failure with Lewontin's seedlings argument attached. Going further would have turned a map about measurement processes into an argument about behaviour genetics, which is a different project and a much longer one.
    • Cyril Burt. The twin-study fraud allegations are genuinely contested and the evidence is tangled enough that including it responsibly would have cost more space than the point was worth. Larry P. / PASE makes the “careful people reach opposite conclusions” point more cleanly.
    • Multiple-intelligences and EQ frameworks. Popular, and mostly a stage-one dispute about the construct that never reaches allocation. Low stakes, so by the map's own argument, not where the danger is.
    • Nearly all of the fairness-metrics literature after 2017. One theorem does the work; a survey of the subsequent metric zoo would have buried it.
    • School and workplace testing outside the UK and US. A real gap, and the reason the map claims less generality than it might appear to. The pipeline should apply anywhere; I have only checked it in two countries.

    Where I had to be careful with the evidence

    A project about the ethics of evidence does not get to be careless with evidence, so several things are marked contested rather than resolved, including some where the tidier version would have suited my argument better:

    • Whether intelligence testing actually drove the 1924 Immigration Act. Gould said yes; Snyderman & Herrnstein went through the congressional record and found it barely came up. Unresolved, and the strong version would have made my case look better.
    • Gould on Morton. The most-cited critic of biased measurement was accused, on re-measurement, of biased measurement — and that accusation was then disputed in turn. Including it was uncomfortable and clearly correct; exempting the map's favourite critic would have been the exact failure the map is about.
    • The Flynn effect reversal, and the causes of the original rise. The effect is solid; the explanation is not, and the map does not borrow certainty it does not have.

    Three things I changed my mind about

    1. The map needed the corrections, or it was propaganda. My first outline was failures only. That version argues that measurement is bad, which is both false and useless — and it cannot explain Gender Shades, where a benchmark was the instrument that made a harm visible, or the Flynn effect, where the tests undercut the claims made for them. The correction markers are what make it an argument rather than a complaint.
    2. COMPAS is not an intelligence test, and belongs anyway. I cut it twice on scope grounds. It earns its place because it is the purest modern case of the pipeline reaching allocation, and because the impossibility result attached to it constrains every other row on the map. The panel says plainly that it is an adjacent case rather than pretending otherwise.
    3. Binet is the control condition, not the origin story. I had him as “where it all started.” He is more useful as the counterfactual: the caveats were published, in the instrument's own documentation, by its author — and they were gone within a decade anyway. The same thing happened to Turing's explicit statement that he was substituting a different question. Stating your limits demonstrably does not protect them, which is a much more uncomfortable finding than “they should have known better,” and it is the finding that makes the machine column worrying rather than reassuring.

    On the two axes of the second view

    The vertical axis asks what the score decides and is fairly objective. The horizontal axis is my assessment against a stated five-question rubric, and reasonable people would move several points. I have put the rubric next to the chart rather than in a footnote, because a project arguing that construct validity matters should show its own. The argument survives large disagreements about individual placements — it is about the shape of the occupied region, not about any one point.

    How generative AI was used

    The thesis, the seven stages, the decision to organise by stage rather than by harm, the two-band layout, the choice of which cases make the argument and which were cut, and the stakes and rubric axes are mine. I used an AI assistant to draft and refine the case and marker prose against sources I specified, to push back on classifications and on where I was overstating, and to implement the rendering, filtering and interaction. I worked in pieces — stages first, then cases, then markers, then the second view — so each conceptual choice was settled before anything was built on top of it.
