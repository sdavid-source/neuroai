Borrowed Brains

What AI actually took from neuroscience — and what it only pretends to

The claim: every statement of the form “X in AI is brain-inspired” is really one of five different claims, and they are not worth the same. This map types each one.

“Brain-inspired” does an enormous amount of unearned work in AI writing. It covers Fukushima reading Hubel & Wiesel and building their hierarchy on purpose; it covers a 2017 architecture that happens to reuse a word from 1980s cognitive psychology; and it covers cases where the influence ran the other way entirely and an engineering algorithm became our best theory of a brain region. Collapsing those into one phrase is how you end up believing that transformers tell you something about attention.

So the unit of this map is the edge, not the node. Each connection is classified, argued for, and given its strongest counter-argument. Read the colours before you read the boxes.

How I built this, and what I left out

The organising choice

I started from a much longer list of “brain-inspired AI” pairings and kept running into the same problem: the interesting variable was never the pairing itself, it was what kind of relationship it was. Convolution really does descend from Hubel & Wiesel by a traceable paper trail. Transformer attention does not descend from Treisman at all — the mechanism arrived under a different name and was renamed afterwards. Both get described in the same three words.

So I made the classification the backbone of the map rather than a footnote on it. The five types — import, reverse transfer, convergent discovery, loose inspiration, false cognate — are a taxonomy I built for this project, not one I found in a paper. That is the part of the map I would most expect someone to argue with, which is why every edge carries its own counter-argument: the classification is a claim, and claims should be contestable.

The layout is an argument

Two columns with time running downward was chosen so that three things are visible simultaneously: which field an idea belongs to, when it happened, and therefore which way an edge points. An edge that runs upward from right to left is doing something conceptually different from one running downward from left to right — you can see reverse transfers without reading a single label. Faint same-column edges are within-field descent, drawn deliberately dimmer so that cross-field traffic stays visually dominant.

What I cut, and why

  • Everything about consciousness. Global workspace theory, integrated information — the transfers there are almost all metaphor, and one map cannot argue two theses.
  • Most of comparative cognition. Genuinely interesting, but it connects to the neuroscience column, not the transfer story.
  • Spiking network training methods (surrogate gradients, ANN-to-SNN conversion). This is where the action is in neuromorphic learning, but it would have doubled the node count to sharpen one edge.
  • Reinforcement learning after DQN. The reverse-transfer story is fully made by the dopamine and distributional-dopamine edges; policy gradients and model-based RL add scale, not argument.
  • Scaling laws and parameter counts. Deliberately excluded. Comparing parameters to synapses presupposes exactly the false cognate that the “two things called a neuron” edge exists to attack.

Three things I changed my mind about while researching

  1. Neuromorphic engineering belonged on the map. I nearly cut it as a dead end. Then I realised it is the map's most important counterexample: it is the most biologically faithful import of all, and it lost. If “more brain-like is better” were true, that edge should have won.
  2. The grid-cell result is contested, and the contest is the point. I had it filed as a clean convergent-discovery success until I read Schaeffer, Khona & Fiete's reply showing the emergence depends heavily on architectural and regularisation choices. If you tune until grids appear, you found them; the network did not. That caution generalises to every “the network learned brain-like representations” headline, so it earned a permanent warning on the edge.
  3. Backprop-as-a-question is itself a transfer. I originally drew the weight-transport objection as pure neuroscience. It is not — it only exists because an engineering algorithm set the agenda. Framing the biological question as “how does the brain approximate backprop” presupposes that the brain should want to, and that presupposition is a reverse transfer worth marking.

How generative AI was used

The thesis, the five-type taxonomy, the choice of which edges make the argument and which were cut, the two-column-plus-time layout, and the scorecard's five constraints are mine. I used an AI assistant to draft and refine the node and edge prose against sources I specified, to argue with me about classifications (the predictive coding → self-supervised learning edge moved from “import” to “loose inspiration” because of one of those arguments), and to implement the rendering, pan/zoom and filtering. I worked in pieces — data model, then layout, then interaction, then the scorecard — rather than asking for a finished site, so that each conceptual choice was made before anything was built on top of it.
