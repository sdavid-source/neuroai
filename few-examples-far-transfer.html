Few Examples, Far Transfer

Every learner brings something to a problem before it sees a single example — a shape built in by a designer, an instinct shaped by evolution, or structure picked up from earlier data. Call that a prior. Priors buy speed. This map asks what they charge for it.

Priors that are built or evolved buy speed by narrowing — they make a learner fast at one problem and useless at the rest. Priors learned from data appear not to. That difference, rather than one universal tradeoff, is what the map shows.

Each dot is one learner. Colour and shape say which family it belongs to.

Right means it needs fewer examples. Up means what it learned is useful in more places.

What the words mean

Prior
Anything a learner already has before it starts: a designer's rules, an instinct, or structure carried over from earlier training.
Near and far transfer
Near means new problems of the same shape. Far means a different kind of problem altogether. Far is much rarer.
Critical period
A window early in life when something can be learned easily, which closes and does not reopen.
Pretraining
The very large, general training run a model does before anyone asks it to do a specific task.
Fine-tuning
Adjusting an already-pretrained model with a small set of examples for one job.
In-context learning
A model picking up a new task from a few examples written into the prompt, with no training at all.
Correlation (r)
How strongly two things move together, from −1 to +1. Zero means no relationship. Negative means one goes up as the other goes down.

What the toggle does

Lifetime

Counts only the examples this learner met during the episode in question. Evolution and pretraining are treated as free.

Total

Counts the whole budget: evolution for animals, pretraining for models, hand-built knowledge for symbolic systems.

The ones that move furthest are the ones that looked most impressive before — the newborn face bias, taste aversion, the digger wasp, in-context learning. Things that looked unrelated end up in the same place: taste aversion, the wasp and birdsong all land on one square, and Deep Blue lands on AlphaZero.

That collision is worth seeing, but the accounting rule forces it, so it illustrates the idea rather than testing it. The test is in the next section.

Does the map show what it claims?

Worth checking rather than assuming. Correlate the two axes across all 36 points and the answer is no: r = +0.06 under lifetime accounting, +0.23 under total. A tradeoff would be negative. On that reading there is no tradeoff here at all.

Split the points by where the prior came from and they separate. Use the Prior filter above to see the two groups on the map. Under lifetime accounting:

Built or evolved · 26 points

r = −0.12. Slightly negative, the predicted direction. The wasp, taste aversion, birdsong and Bayesian Program Learning are all fast and go nowhere.

Learned from data · 9 real systems

r = +0.56. Positive, the opposite direction. Pretraining, fine-tuning and in-context learning get speed and breadth together.

The gap between those is the finding. It is a better claim than a universal tradeoff because it could have failed, and for one of the two groups it did.

How the split is drawn, since everything rests on it. The question is where a system's domain knowledge came from. Deep Blue counts as built, because grandmasters wrote its evaluation terms even though the weights were tuned on games. AlphaZero counts as learned, because nothing about chess beyond the rules was supplied. The image models are the arguable ones — a convolutional network carries a designed prior about images, and it is classed as learned only because the content it recognises came from the data. Reasonable people would move those. The filter lets you check what happens if you do.

Three things keep the number honest. ARC-AGI is excluded from the +0.56: its coordinates are an aspiration rather than a measurement, and including it inflates the figure to +0.70. The result also leans on a few points — drop in-context learning and it falls to +0.24. And the coordinates are one person's rankings, so this describes the map rather than the world. Several biological points ignore the pattern outright: infant segmentation at 9 and 6, the toddler at 8 and 7, the honeybee at 5 and 7 are high on both axes with priors nobody designed.

Split the humans out of the built group and it separates again. Humans on their own give r = +0.09 — no tradeoff. Animals and hand-built systems give r = −0.39 — a clear one. That reproduces a known result inside this map. Chang and colleagues trained children for five days and found the faster learners also did better on new problems: faster and broader went together. They changed the learner and held the priors fixed; this map changes the priors. Among people sharing the same equipment, learning fast does not make you narrow. Across systems built on different priors, it does. One caveat travels with it — Chang measured transfer to problems of the same shape, and this map mostly asks about transfer across domains.

Under total accounting the built group flips to +0.53, the wrong way. That is not a discovery. Total accounting crushes almost everything into the left margin, where the axis stops telling a wasp apart from a trillion-token training run.

The human points are an age series

Filter to Human and turn on Ages. Species is held constant, and in the two language cases the task is too, so only the age of first exposure changes.

The prior arrives already installed at birth. Speed peaks in infancy. Between six and twelve months an infant loses the sounds its language does not use — a general ability spent to get better at one language, which is the only place on this map where the trade can be watched rather than inferred. A child picks up a second language to native level without trying; an adult with more intelligence and more effort does not. And people who meet their first language late never reach native competence, however many decades follow.

Speed in humans is not a fixed trait that fades. It is a window that opens, narrows onto whatever arrives, and shuts.

ARC-AGI is drawn as a target, not a result

ARC-AGI-2 tests whether a system can pick up a genuinely new task from two or three examples. It is a dashed ring rather than a dot because it marks where the corner would be if something reached it, and it disappears under total accounting, since an aspiration has no experience budget.

The scores are contested and move fast, so treat any figure as something to re-check. As of September 2026 frontier models report around 90% on the ARC-AGI-2 public leaderboard, well above that benchmark's human panel average of 60% — while the same benchmark under a strict compute cap sits near 24%. That gap is the interesting part. If a system beats the human average only by spending enormous computation per puzzle, the cost did not vanish; it moved from training examples to thinking time. Whether that belongs here as a third axis is the strongest open question on this map.

Positions are arguments, not measurements. The numbers are rankings, and the units do not really compare across families — nobody can say a crow needed n examples the way a network did. Every point names a study so the placement can be argued with, and shaky ones carry a dashed ring.

Why a space and not a ranking. Poirier and colleagues reanalysed 555 correlations between cognitive abilities in animals and found an average of r = 0.185. If abilities do not collapse onto one line, a league table is the wrong object. Chollet makes the same move for machines, and keeps priors and experience as separate terms — which is exactly what the toggle here merges. That merge works as a way of making a point, not as a measurement.

Three objections, and the answers. First, the transfer axis mixes up how many domains a skill covers with how far it reaches inside one. AlphaFold scores 2 for generalising to proteins unlike anything it trained on; the honeybee scores 7 for carrying one rule between two senses. The fix is to define transfer by how far an input can drift before performance drops, which would raise AlphaFold sharply and drop riding a bike to zero.

Second, what would prove this wrong? The universal version could not be — any fast learner could be rescued by moving its cost to evolution or pretraining. The narrower version predicts a difference between two kinds of prior, and that difference can fail to appear. The clean test: two systems with the same total experience, one with a stronger built-in bias. If the stronger one transfers just as far, the claim is false.

Third, some animals may be underrated by their own experiments — tested outside their main sense, or in a lab rather than the setting the behaviour evolved for. Six animal cards now say which, in both directions.
