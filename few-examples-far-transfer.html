Few Examples, Far Transfer

Efficiency is bought with priors. The question worth asking is what the prior charges. Hand-built and evolved priors buy it by narrowing — they work on one problem and nowhere else. Priors learned from data appear not to. That asymmetry, rather than a universal tradeoff, is what this map turns out to be evidence for.

What the toggle is doing

Lifetime

Counts only the examples this individual or model met during the learning episode in question. Evolutionary history and pretraining are treated as free.

Total

Counts the whole experience budget: evolutionary shaping for organisms, pretraining for models, hand-engineered priors for symbolic systems.

Two-thirds of the points move left when you switch — 24 of 36 — and the ones that move furthest are exactly the ones that looked most impressive under lifetime accounting: the newborn face bias, taste aversion, the digger wasp, Deep Blue, in-context learning. In-context learning is the crux: under lifetime accounting it sits alone in the empty corner; under total accounting it slides left and lands on top of pretraining. Same system, opposite conclusions, depending only on what you agree to count.

Watch what collides in the lower left when you switch. Taste aversion, the digger wasp and birdsong land on the same square — three one-trial specialists whose efficiency was paid for by evolution. Deep Blue lands on AlphaZero, having reached the same game from the opposite end. In-context learning lands on pretraining, with fine-tuning arriving at the same efficiency by a different route. Be careful what you claim for the pile-up, though. The accounting rule guarantees it: anything whose efficiency came from a large prior is pushed into the same corner by definition, so the collision illustrates the idea rather than evidencing it. The evidence is the split in the next section, where the same rule applied to two classes of prior gives opposite answers.

Does the map show its own thesis?

Worth checking rather than assuming. Take the 37 placements and correlate the two axes. Across every point at once the answer is no: r = +0.06 under lifetime accounting and +0.23 under total. A tradeoff would be negative. On that reading the thesis is simply not in the data.

Split the points by where the prior came from, though, and the picture separates cleanly. Under lifetime accounting:

Hand-built or evolved priors · n = 27

r = −0.13. Weakly negative, in the direction the thesis predicts. The wasp, taste aversion, birdsong, absolute pitch and Bayesian Program Learning all sit high on efficiency and at or near zero on transfer.

Priors learned from data · n = 10

r = +0.70. Strongly positive, the opposite direction. Pretraining, fine-tuning and in-context learning get breadth and efficiency together, and nothing on this side pays the narrowing tax.

The gap between −0.13 and +0.70 is the finding, and it is a better one than the universal tradeoff it replaces, because it is a claim that could have come out the other way and for one of the two groups it did. Three caveats keep it honest. A correlation of −0.13 across 27 points is weak, not a result. The coordinates are one author's ordinal judgements, so this describes the placements rather than measuring the world — treating it as independent evidence would be circular. And a handful of biological points resist it outright: infant statistical learning at 9 and 6, the toddler at 8 and 7, the honeybee at 5 and 7 are all high on both axes with priors nobody designed.

One more thing the split exposes. Under total accounting the hand-built group flips to r = +0.52 — the wrong way. That is not a discovery, it is compression: total accounting pushes almost everything into the left margin, where the efficiency axis stops discriminating between a wasp and a trillion-token training run. The toggle is a good rhetorical device and a blunt measuring instrument, and it should not be asked to be both.

The developmental arc

Filter the map to Human and switch Ages on, and the family reads as one series from birth to old age. It is the cleanest control on the map: species held constant, and in the two language cases the task held constant too, with only the age of first exposure varying.

birth

The prior arrives pre-installed

A newborn tracks a face-like pattern having seen no faces. Zero examples is not impressive learning — it is no learning at all. Every point after this one pays for something this one got free.

8 months

Peak efficiency, and unusually broad

Two minutes of unbroken synthetic speech and an infant has the word boundaries. The same statistical machinery works on tones and on visual sequences, which makes this one of the few genuinely high-efficiency, wide-transfer points anywhere on the map.

6 to 12 months

The transaction, visible

At six months an infant hears every language's contrasts. By twelve it has lost the ones its own language ignores. Nothing was added — general capacity was spent to buy efficiency in one language. This is the thesis happening inside one individual in six months.

under 7

Attainment without effort

A child immersed in a second language reaches native-like grammar and accent with no instruction. The window is still open and the priors are still soft.

adult

More intelligence, less learning

The adult learner has strategies, motivation and metacognition the child lacks, and still does not get there. Whatever bought the child's efficiency was never general intelligence.

late, and 65+

The window matters more than the budget

Late first-language learners do not reach native competence after decades of daily use. Meanwhile older learners acquire more slowly but integrate into existing expertise more readily — so the age effect runs in opposite directions on the two axes.

The series makes a stronger claim than any single point could. Sample efficiency in humans is not a stable trait that declines with age — it is a window that opens, narrows onto whatever input arrives, and shuts. Perceptual narrowing and late first-language acquisition are the two ends of that: one shows the prior being installed, the other shows what it costs to arrive after installation is over. If you want the developmental version of your thesis in one line, that is it.

Seven pairs worth putting on a slide

Single points make weak arguments. Pairs make strong ones, because the second point controls for what the first one leaves ambiguous. These are the comparisons this map exists to support.

Child L2 ↔ Adult L2

The tightest control on the map. Identical task, identical species, only the age of first exposure differs — and the child reaches native-like competence while the adult, with every cognitive advantage, does not.

Perceptual narrowing at 6 months ↔ at 12 months

The same infant, six months apart, now unable to hear a contrast it could hear before. The only case here where you can watch breadth being spent to buy efficiency rather than infer it.

Taste aversion ↔ Light-and-nausea

One trial, hours of delay, a memory for years. Then swap taste for a flashing light and the same rat cannot learn it at all. The efficiency was never a general capacity; it was a pre-wired expectation.

Deep Blue ↔ AlphaZero

One needed no examples because grandmasters wrote the knowledge down. The other needed tens of millions because nobody did. Under total accounting they end up in the same corner.

Human one-shot ↔ Bayesian Program Learning

Identical task, identical one-shot performance, two routes. One route's priors were hand-engineered and can be read off the page, which is why this is the cleanest evidence on the map.

Learning to drive ↔ Self-driving cars

Tens of hours against billions of miles, for the same task, and the human still handles the long tail better. The most legible version of the sample-efficiency gap for a general audience.

Riding a bike ↔ A bike with reversed steering

Learned in an afternoon, kept for fifty years, and worth nothing the moment the handlebars turn the other way. Extreme efficiency and retention, bought with a completely rigid representation.

Three regions

The lower-left region has become the crowded one, and that is worth saying out loud in a presentation: most of the AI people actually touch every day lives down there — the recommender choosing what plays next, the classifier reading a digit, the car. Cold start, the recommender industry's most expensive open problem, is this map's x-axis asked in production.

The boundary condition: within a population, faster learners transfer better

Chang et al. trained children on a set of problems for five days and found that individual differences in learning rate predicted transfer — the faster learners showed the greater gains on novel problems. Faster and broader went together. That is the opposite of a tradeoff, and it is the first thing a careful reader will bring up.

It is not a refutation, it is a limit on the claim, and stating the limit makes the thesis sharper. Chang varied the learner while holding the priors fixed; this map varies the priors. Within one population sharing the same equipment, whatever makes a child learn quickly also makes them generalise well, so efficiency and transfer are positively related. Across systems built on different priors, they trade off. Both can be true, and the map is only making the second claim.

One caveat to carry with it: what Chang measured was near transfer, to novel but similarly structured problems. This map's y-axis mostly asks about far transfer, across domains. Those are different quantities, and the map does not currently distinguish them — which is the same conflation the AlphaFold point runs into from the other direction.

Open problem: ARC-AGI has moved, and it moved in a way the map has to answer for

ARC-AGI is drawn here as a dashed target zone rather than a dot, because it marks where the corner would be if something got there. As of September 2026 the scores are contested and the leaderboards disagree, so treat every figure below as something to re-check rather than cite:

  • ARC Prize's own verified leaderboard — the one that also reports dollars — had the top verified commercial model at roughly 37.6% for $2.20 per task.
  • Third-party aggregators tracking the public frontier-API leaderboard report far higher, in the 85–92% band, on runs that are not cost-verified.
  • The compute-capped Kaggle track, which forbids internet APIs and caps spend near $50 a submission, was at 24% on the 2025 private set.
  • The human panel, over 400 participants, averages 60% at about $17 a task.

The spread between those rows is the finding. If frontier systems clear the human panel average while burning orders of magnitude more inference compute per task, the tradeoff this map argues for did not disappear — it relocated, from training examples to test-time compute. ARC Prize plots score against cost per task for exactly that reason, which is a close cousin of this figure with a third axis substituted in. Deciding whether test-time compute belongs on your map, as a third dimension or as a third accounting mode, is the strongest move available to this project.

Positions are arguments, not measurements. The axes are ordinal and the units are not commensurable across families — nobody can say a crow needed n examples in the same sense a network did. Every point is anchored to a specific study so the placement can be contested; low-confidence placements carry a dashed ring on the map and a note in the panel.

Before publishing, verify: trial counts and sample sizes in each cited study (these were drafted from memory), the cross-modal claim in the honeybee point, the practice-hour figure for learning to drive, the older-adult point (which has no specific study behind it yet), current self-driving fleet mileage, and the ARC-AGI figures above. On the total axis, the low end is heavily compressed — a wasp's evolutionary budget and a trillion-token pretraining run both land near zero, which the ordinal scale cannot separate.

Why a plane and not a ranking. Poirier et al. reanalysed 555 bivariate correlations among cognitive abilities in non-human animals and found an average of r = 0.185 — weak support for a general factor. If abilities do not collapse onto one axis in animals, a league table is the wrong object and a space of tradeoffs is the right one. Chollet makes the matching move for machines, defining intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency relative to priors, experience and generalisation difficulty — note that he keeps priors and experience as separate terms, which is exactly what the total-accounting toggle here merges into one. That merge is this map's biggest liberty. It is defensible as a rhetorical device and not as a measurement, which is why the correlation section above reports the lifetime split rather than the total one.

Three objections, and what this map answers.

The transfer axis conflates breadth of domain with reach within one. True, and it is the map's real weakness. AlphaFold generalises to proteins unlike anything in its training set and still scores 2, because proteins are one domain; the honeybee scores 7 for carrying one rule across two modalities inside a single foraging paradigm. The axis is rewarding the kind of generality rather than its extent. The fix is an operational definition — how far can an input move from the training distribution before performance degrades — under which AlphaFold rises sharply, riding a bike falls to zero, and the other-race effect becomes a measurement rather than a caveat. That is the next version of this map, and until it exists the transfer coordinates should be read as claims about kind.

What would falsify it? The universal version was unfalsifiable, which is why it is gone: any efficient learner could be rescued by relocating its cost to evolution or pretraining, and a claim that survives every case by moving the goalposts is not a claim. The narrowed version pays its way, because it predicts a difference between two classes of prior and that difference could fail to appear. It nearly does — the biological counterexamples are named above. The clean test is matched-budget prior manipulation: two systems with equal total experience, one with a stronger inductive bias, and if the stronger-prior system transfers at least as far, the claim is false. Lake and Baroni's meta-learning result is the most likely place to look for that, since a learned prior there buys systematic generalisation rather than spending it.

Some animals may be underrated by their own experiments. Bräuer et al. argue species are routinely tested outside their primary modality — dogs studied through vision and hearing when olfaction is their main channel — so a low transfer score can be a fact about the study rather than the animal. This map does not correct for that and cannot. Treat every animal point as a lower bound, and treat the rats-beat-humans point as the warning: put a species in a task matched to its ecology and the ordering can invert. Which domains count as "far" apart is a cultural judgement too, as Sternberg's examples of competence outside the classroom make plain.
